# Morning Brief

Your morning brief for what's happening in your environment & how to respond
to it. Unlike other weather apps that give you data to interpret (humidity,
dew point, UV index) this one tells you what the weather will feel like & how
you should prepare or respond. It keeps track of when you need an umbrella,
sure, but also what shoes to wear, if you need sunscreen, if you should bring
a jacket, if it is a good night for stargazing, very simple steps you can
take to help birds and pollinators, and even if you'll want to plan extra
time in the morning to scrape the ice off of your car. There's so much more,
too.

One static HTML file. No server, no build step, no account.
Open it and it works.

**Live:** https://rhymes-with-tree.github.io/weather/

---

## What it does

**Four cards run across the day** as a gradient from midnight to midnight —
Feels Like, Sky, Sunrise and Sunset, and the Moon — so the middle of the card
is the middle of the day, and a glance at where you are on the gradient tells
you where you are in the day.

**A grid of tiles** for conditions that are genuinely out there right now:
Air Quality, Allergens, Sunburn, Water Temperature, Pavement, Wind, Aurora,
plus a growing set that only appear when they apply — Migraine, Arthritis,
Frostbite, Bugs and Hair Frizz among them, each carrying a band table
explaining what the number actually means rather than just a label. A tile is
something the weather (or the sky, or a government order) is *doing*; most
vanish entirely on an ordinary day. Notable conditional ones: an eclipse
today, a meteor shower near its peak, a flag at half-staff, elevated
respiratory viruses in your state's wastewater, bird migration season, and —
with your own free key — notable bird sightings nearby.

**A to-do list**, grouped into Planning your day, Heading out, and Planning
your night — every pill is generated by the same card that would show a tile,
so migraine meds, what shoes to wear, whether to drip the faucets, and
whether to fly a kite all collect into one list instead of requiring fourteen
separate taps. Each pill leads with the one word that matters, bold, with any
reason underneath in smaller type. Tap a pill once it's handled and it fades.

**Later this week** works differently on purpose: day-grouped, always-visible
text, not pills — a small heading per day, plain sentences underneath, ready
to read at a glance rather than something to tap through. It scans the next
six days for a migraine or arthritis score that's both high and clearly worse
than today, the first overnight freeze, a temperature swing worth planning
around, or an eclipse. A separate check looks specifically at *tomorrow
morning* — frost, mowing before it gets too hot, an early dog walk, a clear
morning light window — since by the time any of those would show up as
"today," it's already too late to plan around them.

**A 3, 5, or 7-day view** for the four continuous cards, switchable between
gradient strips spanning the whole range or discrete pills per day — day
headers double as jump links into the full single-day page for that date.

**"Right now"**, when something is actually falling — rain, snow, a storm —
read from Open-Meteo's separate current-conditions estimate rather than the
day's forecast rollup, so a shower the forecast didn't predict still shows up
somewhere on the page.

Settings turn off whatever doesn't apply to you — no lawn, no car, no
basement, no dog, conditions you don't have, or a whole category if you'd
rather not see it. Four colour themes are built in, including a
colourblind-safe one, plus custom colour pickers if none of them fit.

**On BirdCast specifically:** their API is confirmed CORS-blocked from a
browser — tested directly against this app's own deployed origin with
devtools, not assumed from documentation — so the bird migration card links
to their forecast rather than fetching it. The real endpoint and payload
shape are known (see the project's own TODO for the investigation notes);
direct integration just isn't possible without Cornell adding this origin to
an allowlist.

When Mast data is on screen, the flag card carries a *Powered by Mast* credit
linking back to them, as they ask. Nothing is shown when no Mast key is set.

Open-Meteo's data is CC BY 4.0 and requires attribution. The app credits every
source in its own About panel as well as here.

Nothing is proxied. Every request goes straight from the visitor's browser to
the services above.

## Optional API keys

Three, all pasted in Settings → API Key, all stored in the visitor's own
browser, all billed to whoever pasted them — hosting this costs the host
nothing either way.

**Mast**, for state flag orders. Leave it blank and the half-staff card
covers federal orders and the dates set by law only. Every governor issues
their own orders in their own format, so there's no single free source for
those — Mast maintains a reader per state. This is the easiest of the three
to set up: an email address, no payment method, and the key arrives by email.
Their free tier is 250 requests a month; this app uses at most four a day,
per state, cached for six hours.

**eBird**, for notable bird sightings nearby. Leave it blank and the Notable
Birds tile simply doesn't appear — there's no fallback source for "rare bird
nearby" the way CAMS backs up Google's air quality. Free and simple: create
an eBird account if you don't have one already (it's the same account used
across all the Cornell Lab apps), then request a key at
[ebird.org/api/keygen](https://ebird.org/api/keygen).

**Google Maps Platform**, for measured pollen and finer-grid air quality.
Leave it blank and allergens come from a regional bloom calendar and air
quality from CAMS at roughly 45 km. Add a key with the Pollen and Air Quality
APIs enabled and you get measured pollen with named plants, plus air quality
on a 500 m grid with health guidance written for respiratory conditions.
This is the most involved of the three to set up — it goes through Google
Cloud Console rather than a simple signup form, with billing enabled even
though a personal bill should be zero at this app's usage (5,000 free calls
a month per API, cached for 30 minutes, a handful of calls a day in
practice).

Each card says which source it used either way.

## Privacy

There is no account and no server. Settings, saved places and API keys live
in the visitor's browser via `localStorage`. Coordinates go to the weather
services above as part of each request, and nowhere else. The site uses
[GoatCounter](https://www.goatcounter.com) for basic visit counts —
cookieless, no personal data collected, aggregate only — and it's the one
thing here worth calling tracking even in the loosest sense of the word. One
more exception: if a Mast key is set and a state order is showing, opening
the About panel loads one font from Google so the credit renders in Mast's
own wordmark.

## Honest limits

- **US only.** Units are imperial throughout, NWS alerts are US-only, the air
  quality index is US EPA, the bloom calendars cover six US regions, and the
  half-staff card only knows about federal law and (with a key) US states.
- **Most health cards are heuristics**, not validated instruments. Migraine,
  arthritis, bugs, frizz, shoes and mowing are rules written by hand from
  commonly cited triggers. The arthritis link in particular is widely reported
  and weakly evidenced — several large studies found little or no association.
- **Allergens without a Google key are modelled, not counted.** A regional
  bloom calendar adjusted for wind, humidity and rain. Cross-check a station
  count before trusting it.
- **Viral activity is state-level, not local.** Genuinely measured, not
  modelled, but it describes a whole state's wastewater and says nothing about
  your own risk.
- **Water temperature** tries an NDBC buoy first, then a NOAA CO-OPS shoreline
  gauge, then a modelled sea surface temperature. NDBC does not send CORS
  headers, so in a browser it is normally the CO-OPS gauge that answers. The
  card names its source and how far away it is; on a big lake, twenty miles of
  shore can differ by ten degrees after an upwelling.
- **"Right now" is a model estimate, not radar**, updated roughly hourly by
  Open-Meteo. It can genuinely disagree with the day's forecast headline above
  it — that's the point of having both, not a bug in either.
- **Solar eclipses get a heads-up, not a promise.** Knowing whether one exact
  point on Earth falls inside a path a few hundred miles wide needs real
  shadow geometry this app doesn't attempt; it names the event and links to
  NASA's own calculator instead. Lunar eclipses, visible from the whole night
  side of Earth, get real computed local circumstances. The table is
  hand-maintained through 2032 and needs a manual refresh after that.
- **Meteor shower peak dates are fixed calendar days, not computed.** They
  shift by roughly a day year to year, so these are accurate to about ±1 day
  and worth a spot-check against AMS if a shower ever looks off.
- **Ice safety is a recent-cold nudge, not an ice-thickness reading.** A real
  estimate needs freezing-degree-days summed over a whole season; this app
  only has a few days of forecast history to look back on. It flags three or
  more consecutive days at or below freezing and always says to check
  locally, on purpose.
- **Tick risk has no live source anywhere free.** CDC and state health data
  are retrospective annual case counts, not something to query for today's
  risk — this is a season-and-temperature rule, not a measurement.
- **Bird migration is a link, not a forecast this app makes itself** — see
  the Sources note on BirdCast above.
- **The flag card is federal-only without a Mast key.** Presidential
  proclamations also reach the Federal Register a day or more after signing,
  so a very recent order may not appear yet even with a key.
- Thresholds are constants at the top of each card function and are meant to be
  tuned against your own record.

## Not medical advice

This page cannot tell you how you will feel. Don't use it to make decisions
about medication, treatment, or whether to seek care. For weather that could
hurt you, the National Weather Service is the authority. Water conditions in
particular change faster than any forecast — check local flags and signage
before you swim.
